## 2. Decisions taken

- **Shelve FTC-TP under taker execution.** Not deployable cross-the-spread. Data-supported subset isn't cleaner — it's the subset where execution is most expensive (5-7¢ entry slip observed vs 3¢ assumed) because markets that print follow-up fills are markets where price was actively drifting.
- **Revise under WS-passive execution.** Posting passive limits at the touch via CLOB WebSocket bypasses the next-fill proxy entirely — the proxy was measuring what *other takers* paid, not what your posted limit would fill at. Under WS-passive, the right metric is **fill rate × exact-price math**, not next-fill slippage. Outcome: **`(p_in=0.50, p_out=0.90)` is deployable** at +6% ROI per filled trade, 2,283 fills/yr (~6/day), optimistic-exit assumption. Strict-exit assumption flips to -2.5% on canonical (0.60, 0.90) — so real performance is determined by queue priority on the exit ask. **Recommend small-live 2-4 weeks** to measure actual fill mix before scaling.
- **Execution-model assumption is now a first-class research input** — not an afterthought to the slippage proxy. The taker-vs-passive pivot moved the strategy from "shelve" to "deployable" without changing a single data point. Document the execution model alongside any deployability verdict from now on.
- **Slippage diagnostic must call out its own untrustworthiness.** Adopted pattern: emit `"constant_slippage_with_labelling"` flag when fallback_pct > 50%. Apply to other slippage modules going forward. **Scope clarification**: this and the Proposal B "data-supported subsets are more expensive" finding both apply under *taker* execution. Under passive execution, "data-supported subsets" is just a description of where fills happen.

## 3. Bugs / surprising lessons

**Compounding bug — 4 orders of magnitude error.** `equity = cumprod(1+ret)` while `pnl_pct` was computed on a flat $1 base. Backtest reported total_return = 2,272,346% (read out loud: two million percent). Real number: 628.6%. **Lesson:** when sharing capital across trades, decide once whether you're modelling compounding or flat-bankroll, and make sure the equity curve and the per-trade PnL series use the same convention. The two are mathematically incompatible. Subtle because Sharpe and bucket probabilities are unaffected — only the wealth-trajectory numbers blow up.

**Silent NaN coercion to defaulted label.** NaN resolution silently became `0` (chop). 0 affected rows in this dataset, but the latent landmine was there. **Lesson:** defaulted-label coercion of NaN inputs is among the most dangerous silent failures in financial code. Always raise or explicitly drop; never assume the default category is benign.

**Dedup on the wrong timestamp.** `one_trade_per_city` was keying on `entry_ts.floor("D")`; should have been `end_ts.floor("D")`. Two-way drift — caught some legitimate duplicates and missed others (same-day-entry-different-resolution-day siblings). **Lesson:** when deduping bets, dedup on the field that defines the *outcome partition*, not the field that defines convenience. For event-resolution betting that's `end_ts`, not `entry_ts`.

**Default param drift between code paths.** `backtest()` default was `max_notional_pct=0.05`; `grid_backtest` was passing `0.02`; docstring said `0.02`. Inflated `avg_notional_per_trade` from 1.98% to 3.69% on direct `backtest()` calls. **Lesson:** when multiple call sites pass the same param, the function's default is dead code at best and a misalignment bomb at worst. Either remove the default (require callers to pass explicitly) or move the default to a module-level config constant that all call sites read from.

**Per-cell annualisation span breaks grid comparability.** `grid_backtest` was computing Sharpe/CAGR with a different annualisation span per (p_in, p_out) cell — span derived from each cell's first/last trade. Cells with shorter spans had inflated annualised numbers. **Lesson:** when comparing across configurations of the same backtest, the time axis must be a universe-wide constant, not derived per-config. Otherwise you're comparing different things.

**Constant-slippage-with-labelling — slippage proxies dominated by their fallback are not measurements.** When `fallback_pct > 50%` of a leg's next-fill lookups returned no real fill, the resulting "slippage estimate" is just the fallback constant with extra labelling. The diagnostic emits the literal string `"constant_slippage_with_labelling"` to make this explicit. **Lesson:** any imputed value can be reported as a measurement; the test of whether it actually *is* a measurement is whether the answer would change if you swapped the imputation rule. Sensitivity test on `fallback_cents ∈ {1, 2, 3, 5, 8}` is the obvious follow-up.

**Data-supported subsets are not cleaner — they're more expensive.** Restricting weather analysis to crosses where a real next-fill existed in 5 min (29% of anchors) made edge *worse*, not better. The markets that print follow-up fills are exactly the markets where price is drifting (which is why a follow-up exists). Filtering on "next fill exists" introduces selection bias toward higher-drift-cost trades. **Lesson:** when proposing a "filter to data-supported subset" cleanup, also ask: what does the existence of supporting data correlate with? In thin markets with episodic activity, it correlates with adverse selection.

**Wider windows include drift, not execution cost.** Extending the next-fill window from 5min to 10min only dropped fallback by 5-10pp and made edges *worse* (same_dir −0.69¢, opp_dir −0.32¢). Later fills carry drift information, not execution-cost information. **Lesson:** when a slippage proxy gets *worse* as you give it more time, that proxy is contaminated with directional drift. The fix isn't more time — it's a structurally better fill model (e.g. bid/ask quotes, not trade prints).

**Weather markets are structurally thin.** 43% of crosses have NO follow-up fill within 30 minutes. P10/P25/P50/P75/P90/P99 lag (conditional on any fill): 30/86/298/788/1315/1752 seconds. **Lesson:** for any execution strategy on this market class, structural illiquidity dominates the realisable edge. This is a market-class-level finding, not strategy-specific — likely generalises to any sub-second-edge strategy on event markets.
